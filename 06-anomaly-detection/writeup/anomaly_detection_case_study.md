# Ledger anomalies, marked against ground truth

## Flags are cheap; attention is not

The ledger described in this case study is synthetic, built with a known answer key held out of every layer of analysis. No real company sits behind any figure below.

A mid-size company's purchase ledger runs to about fifty thousand rows a year. Nobody reads it, and nobody could – reading every row was never the constrained resource. Producing flags is cheap. A handful of rule tests or a statistical model can raise thousands of them from a ledger this size without much effort. What is scarce is a competent person's time to look at each flag and decide whether it means anything – a duplicate submission, a coding error, a habit of staying just under an approval threshold, or nothing at all. Get that triage wrong in either direction and it costs money. Real process issues compound quietly for months if the flags that would have shown them are buried in noise, and good people burn attention on round numbers and Saturday postings that turn out to mean nothing.

This case tests three different ways of turning a ledger into flags worth someone's time, and marks each one honestly against a set of anomalies planted into the data before any of the three layers ever saw it. The framing throughout is controls and process quality – coding errors, duplicate submissions, threshold habits – never accusation.

## Three layers, one answer key

The ledger belongs to a fictional company, Saarnitukku Oy, a technical wholesale and MRO distributor selling fasteners, seals, tools and maintenance supplies to industrial customers. It runs a Vantaa head office with branches in Tampere and Kuopio, turns over roughly €90 million a year with about 180 staff, and posts its purchase ledger through a four-person accounts-payable team. Procurement peaks every November and December, as customers stock up ahead of their own year-end, and troughs every July.

Three layers screen that ledger, each different in kind. The first is a set of seven deterministic rule tests, each traceable to sourced audit practice – duplicate invoices, round sums, amounts sitting just under the company's €10,000 approval tier, purchases split to slip under that same tier, weekend and holiday postings, mis-posted accounts, and exact-match vendor-name duplication. The second is a statistical anomaly detector, an Isolation Forest running over eight engineered features per transaction, built to catch what a fixed rule set cannot specify in advance. The third is three independent AI analyst runs, each working from an identical pinned briefing and the same public data, blinded to each other's output and to the answer key, and required to explain every flag or stand it down with evidence.

Before any of the three layers saw the ledger, 87 anomalous transactions across eight classes were planted into it, alongside 18 transactions designed to be entirely benign yet certain to attract flags – a landlord's rent that happens to fall on a round number, an agreed Saturday stocktake, a genuine coincidence of two equal invoices from the same vendor. All of it is recorded in a held-out answer key, and every plant and every miss is marked against that key. Nothing that follows is asserted without a number behind it.

## What the rules caught

The rule layer found everything it was built to find. Against the 87 planted anomalies, the seven tests raised 64 flags. Forty-eight of those land on a planted anomaly, a 75% first-pass precision rate and exactly the figure the design set out to hit. Recall on every rule-shaped class – duplicate invoices, round sums and near-threshold amounts, weekend postings, mis-posted accounts, split purchases – is 100%. Every duplicate pair, every split purchase, every misfiled invoice and every weekend posting by the one user who made them was caught.

The other 16 flags land precisely on the ledger's designed benign bank – twelve months of a landlord's rent at an exact, contractual €15,000, two agreed Saturday postings for a stocktake with the memo saying so, and one coincidental pair of identical-amount invoices from the same vendor that turn out, once the purchase-order references are checked, to be two genuinely separate deliveries. A rule test that flags round numbers cannot tell a contract from a habit on its own, and a first pass over this ledger does not try to. That quarter of the flag list is the triage burden the rest of the case exists to test. A practitioner's second pass would clear most of it before anyone has to look – suppressing recurring identical amounts silences the rent flags within a week of use, and a materiality floor removes the smaller round sums that carry no risk regardless of pattern. Those refinements are standard practice rather than something built into this exercise, and they are named here so the rule layer reads as a first pass, not a straw man.

## What the model caught

The Isolation Forest raised exactly 200 flags, the scale the design set out to produce. None of them lands on a planted anomaly. The overlap between the model's 200 flags and the answer key is zero.

This was a pre-registered possibility rather than an after-the-fact excuse. The design committed in advance that if the detector missed its designed catches, the result would be reported and frozen, not retuned until it passed. It missed all three. The vendor whose invoicing quietly drifted upward, which the detector was meant to surface, went uncaught, and so did two designed benign outliers it was meant to flag as unusually sized (though not wrongly, since they are exactly what they look like).

Three causes explain the miss, and each is a genuine limitation of a transaction-grain detector rather than a bug. First, the feature measuring how unusual an invoice is for its own vendor is built from that vendor's full-year average, so a vendor whose whole year drifts upward drags its own yardstick up with it. The one vendor engineered to drift, ending the year about 19% above where it started, reads as only mildly unusual against itself – a peak deviation of about 2.1 standard deviations, where a clean baseline would put the same invoices at around 6.5. A slow-moving problem inflates the average it is measured against. Second, two of the largest one-off purchases in the ledger – a €78,400 machine and a €24,600 annual insurance premium, both entirely legitimate but exactly the size a controller would expect a detector to notice – sit on vendors with a single invoice each. With no purchase history to compare against, the detector had almost nothing to work with beyond the raw amount, and both ranked deep in the ledger (9,069th and 6,641st of 50,000) rather than near the flagged tail. Third, against fifty thousand transactions with naturally wide, heavy-tailed swings in amount, a genuine 19% shift on one vendor barely moves the population-level statistics the detector is actually reading.

Catching the drift anyway would take far more flags than the design ever budgeted for. Reaching its own pre-registered bar – flagging at least six of the drift's twelve elevated invoices – needs a flag list reaching rank 14,667 of 50,000, which is 29% of the entire ledger and not a usable flag list for anyone. A follow-up analysis, built and clearly labelled post-hoc after this result was already known, and never counted against the pre-registered pipeline, recomputed the vendor baseline using only prior invoices instead of the full year. That single change moved the drift's best-ranked invoice from 818th to 98th of 50,000, roughly an eightfold improvement, and for the first time put one of the twelve elevated invoices inside the top 200. That is one invoice, not the six the design called for – a genuine improvement, and still short of the bar. The model's most anomalous output, in the end, was the conclusion that nothing here deserved a second look.

## What the analysts did

All 264 flags from both automated layers were grouped mechanically into 39 verdict units, 13 on the rule side (one per vendor or user carrying a rule flag) and 26 on the detector side, covering the 200 model flags across vendor-level clusters, a top-ten list of the highest individual scores, and one catch-all for everything left over, including the ledger's December procurement peak. Each of the three blinded runs had to call every unit, worry with a named mechanism or stand down with evidence, and the three runs called 37, 35 and 38 of the 39 correctly.

The strongest result sits underneath that headline number. All 26 detector-side units were correctly stood down by all three runs, 78 correct verdicts out of 78 on the model's noise, each backed by recomputed evidence – allowed accounts, within-vendor ranges, no supporting rule flag. That is the triage discipline the case was built to measure, and it held completely. None of the model's 200 statistically-odd-but-innocent flags fooled anyone.

The misses that did happen were plausible readings, not careless ones. One run took six exact-thousand-euro invoices as ordinary contract-style billing rather than the designed round-sum pattern, and the same run took one user's eight weekend and holiday postings as flexible working hours rather than a control question. A different run accepted the "osatoimitus" (partial delivery) wording in three vendors' memos at face value and stood down three split-purchase events that were built specifically to sum to more than the approval tier within days, at exactly three vendors. Each of these is a defensible-sounding call against real memo text, and each is wrong against the key, which is exactly what the marking exists to catch.

Two false positives occurred across the exercise, and both are the same item. Two of the three runs saw a coincidental pair of identical-amount invoices from one vendor, noticed the two different purchase-order references that are the designed evidence they are genuinely separate deliveries, and hedged to a worry verdict anyway, recommending a check against source documents. The evidence was sufficient; caution overrode it. The third run read the same evidence correctly, explicitly contrasting the two distinct references against the shared order reference the ledger's real duplicate pairs carry.

Figure discipline held up well. Across the three runs' findings, 364 numbers were cited as evidence and checked against figures recomputed independently from the raw ledger; none disagreed. Two headline slips were recorded. Two of the three runs stated one vendor's combined spend as roughly €48,001, when their own correctly-stated component figures summed to €49,001.36 – a thousand-euro arithmetic error sitting above numbers that were themselves right.

One run's free hunt went further than the brief required, independently observing that the ledger's largest hand-keyed postings – including the €78,400 capex machine – sit outside both automated layers' reach altogether, and recommending a value-based review. That is a correct, self-derived restatement of the detector's own blind spot, and it was credited as a genuine control observation rather than an overreach.

The standout finding, reached independently by all three runs, is a supplier that exists in the vendor master as three separate records under three near-identical spellings, sharing one VAT number and one address, paid into three different bank accounts, with spend split roughly 7:5:3 across them. No rule test in this design was built to catch it – matching vendor names byte for byte gives it a clean pass – so it surfaced only because each analyst, unprompted, compared vendor records against each other rather than reading the ledger row by row. All three runs made it their top-priority action, and all three gave the same complete mechanism.

## The story nobody caught

This case was built around one story in particular, and no layer found it.

From July, one vendor's invoices begin folding freight charges into the price of the goods themselves – the memos say so, in Finnish, "sis. rahtikulut" ("incl. freight costs") – an eight per cent step up in the invoiced amount. But once freight sits inside the bundled price, a further price rise hides inside the same line – another two per cent a month, compounding, so that by December the vendor is billing about 19% above where the year started. The evidence for this sits in the ledger twice over. It is there in the memo text itself, and again in the freight carrier's own billing on that route, which drops by about €924 per invoice once the delivery portion moves elsewhere, close to the €880 that shifted into the bundled goods price. That mirrored fall would confirm the mechanism to anyone who set the two vendors' invoices side by side.

Nothing caught it. The rule tests were never built to look for a slow price drift, by design. The detector's best-ranked invoice among the twelve affected sat 818th of 50,000, nowhere near its 200-flag cut-off. And each of the three analysts had room in their brief for a bounded, self-directed look at anything in the ledger, and used it elsewhere. A shift worth roughly €1,800 a month on one vendor is easy to miss inside account-level monthly totals; it only shows up when someone compares a vendor's trend against its own history, which is a different exercise from screening a ledger for outliers.

The design also planted one anomaly deliberately built to be uncatchable with this data. Twelve unremarkable monthly invoices from a consulting firm, ordinary in every visible respect, are distinguishable from a real problem only with data this purchase-ledger extract does not carry – contracts, deliverables, a match against goods received. That trap held in both directions. Nothing flagged it, and none of the three analysts accused it without evidence. But the case set out with one class nothing could catch and finished with two, because the vendor whose invoicing quietly changed – the story the whole exercise was built to demonstrate – went unclaimed by rules, model and analysts alike.

## What a production system adds

None of the gaps above are flaws in this design. They are places where a real engagement adds a data source this purchase-ledger extract deliberately leaves out, so the exercise stays honest about what each layer can and cannot see on its own.

Three-way matching – invoice against purchase order against goods receipt – would have caught the ceiling class outright, since an invoice for nothing delivered has nothing to match against; this ledger carries no purchase orders or receipts by design, because that data is exactly what makes the match possible. Vendor-master hygiene keyed on VAT number and IBAN, with fuzzy rather than exact name matching, would have turned the vendor-variant finding into a routine data-quality check instead of something an analyst had to notice unprompted. Recurring-identical suppression and a materiality floor on the rule layer would clear most of the designed benign flags, the contractual rent chiefly, before anyone has to look at them. And a standing per-vendor review, comparing each vendor's spend trend against its own history rather than against the ledger as a whole, is the one method in this case that would have surfaced the freight-and-price story – and it is a report, not a model.

The lesson underneath all three layers, not just the one that missed the most, is to mark a detector and mark an analyst against something known before trusting either. The rule layer's 75% precision, the detector's exact zero overlap with the plant list, and the analysts' 37, 35 and 38 out of 39 are only knowable because a held-out answer key existed for all three to be checked against. A real ledger has no such key. What transfers is the habit of checking a system's output against a reconciled control total, a known incident, or a manual sample, before deciding any of it can be trusted unattended.

## Method note

Every figure in this case study comes from a seeded model (`RANDOM_SEED = 42`), fully regenerable from the published code, with the 87 planted anomalies and 18 benign exceptions recorded in an answer key that stays out of the rule tests, the detector's features, and every file the analysts were allowed to open.

The three analyst runs are Claude Code subagents (Opus), run sequentially and mutually blinded to each other's output and to the answer key, each given an identical pinned briefing and the same seven data files. Every run recorded the full list of files it opened before finishing, and all three lists came back clean – the seven whitelisted inputs, and nothing else. No cost figures are claimed for this layer. The runs took place inside a subscription session rather than as metered API calls, and a metered run remains the honest way to attach a cost figure to a production version of this exercise.

Where 05 Board Reporting works top-down – the variance analysis names the story before anyone goes looking – this case works bottom-up, letting rule tests and a statistical detector surface candidates and reasoning agents explain the ones worth explaining; together the two bracket what "the data storyteller" theme means in practice.

The pipeline itself runs in order from the case study's `code/` folder. `generate_ledger.py` builds the ledger, vendor master, chart of accounts and calendar from the seeded model, alongside the held-out answer key; `validate_ledger.py` checks thirteen coherence rules and exits on any failure; `rule_tests.py` implements the seven audit tests; `detect_anomalies.py` runs the Isolation Forest (200 trees, contamination fixed at 0.4%, eight features per transaction – log amount, a vendor-relative amount z-score, invoice cadence against the vendor's own rhythm, day of week, a non-working-day flag, account rarity within the vendor, month, and a vendor's monthly-volume z-score – seeded for exact reproducibility and verified deterministic by hash comparison across independent runs); `prepare_clusters.py` groups all 264 flags into the 39 verdict units; the three analyst runs, using the pinned briefing at `design/storyteller_briefing.md`, produce the findings files and memos; `evaluate.py` marks all three runs against the answer key mechanically; the adjudicated scorecard this case study draws from, `data/analysis/report.md`, was then written by hand from that worksheet, with every miss left in. The analysis notebook (`notebooks/anomaly_detection_analysis.ipynb`) walks every headline number live from the underlying files, including the contamination ablation and the post-hoc detector variant described above; the interactive page (`interactive/anomaly-detection.html`) steps through the catch matrix and all 39 verdict units, run by run.

Every number above comes from this synthetic ledger and its held-out answer key. No real company, named or composited from real clients, sits behind any of it – the point of building it this way is that the results above can be checked, not simply taken on trust.
